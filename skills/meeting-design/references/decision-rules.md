# Decision Rules

Pick one before the meeting. Say it in the invite. The rule is what people are arguing inside, and when it is unnamed everyone picks a different one.

---

## The selector

Work down this list and stop at the first yes.

1. **Is it genuinely one person's call, and are they in the room?** Directive. Say so before discussion, not after
2. **Does implementation depend on people wanting it, not just complying?** Consensus. Expensive, so be sure
3. **Is it reversible and low cost to undo?** Consent. Fast, with a safety valve
4. **Does one person own the outcome and need cover to decide?** Delegate
5. **Everything else.** Consult. This is the honest default for most business decisions

---

## The five rules

### Directive

**Who decides:** the leader, announced before discussion opens.

**Use when** speed matters more than buy-in, the decision is genuinely theirs, or it has already been made and the meeting exists to explain it.

**Say it like this:** "I have made this call and I want to walk you through the reasoning, then hear what I have missed."

**How it fails:** running a directive decision as though it were a consult. People spend an hour arguing a decision that was already final, discover that afterwards, and stop bringing you their honest read. This does more damage than anything else on this page.

### Consult

**Who decides:** the leader, after hearing everyone.

**Use when** you want the room's information but the accountability is yours. Most business decisions live here.

**Say it like this:** "This is mine to call. I want everyone's view first, and I will decide by Friday and tell you why."

**How it fails:** the leader never circles back with the decision or the reasoning, so contributors conclude the consultation was decoration.

### Consent

**Who decides:** the group, unless someone raises a reasoned objection.

The test is not "do you like this" but "can you see a reason this harms us". That distinction is the whole point, and it is why consent moves faster than consensus.

**Use when** the decision is reversible, the cost of being wrong is low, and you want speed without steamrolling.

**Say it like this:** "I am proposing X. Does anyone have a reason this causes real harm? If not, we go."

**How it fails:** it drifts into consensus because nobody enforces the difference between an objection and a preference.

### Consensus

**Who decides:** everyone, and everyone has to be able to live with the outcome.

**Use when** implementation genuinely depends on willing participation, or the group has to hold together afterwards regardless of the outcome.

**How it fails:** it produces the lowest common denominator, takes several times as long as anything else, and hands a veto to whoever is most stubborn rather than most right. Use it rarely and deliberately.

### Delegate

**Who decides:** one named person, with the group as input.

**Use when** the decision sits with someone who needs visible authority to make it, or when you are deliberately building someone's judgement.

**Say it like this:** "Priya owns this call. We are here to give her what she needs, and whatever she decides is the decision."

**How it fails:** the delegator overrules afterwards. Do that once and the delegation is never believed again.

---

## Two rules that apply whichever you pick

**State it before the discussion, never during.** Naming the rule after a disagreement starts reads as changing the game to win it.

**Say what happens to dissent.** People accept losing a decision far better when they know their objection was recorded. "We are going with X, Sam thinks the risk is Y, and we are accepting that risk" costs one sentence and keeps Sam in the room.
